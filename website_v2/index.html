The Price of Integration

South Africa re-entered the world economy after 1994. Average incomes rose, trade opened, and institutions gained global legitimacy; yet the top 10% now claim about 65% of pre-tax national income while unemployment remains structurally high.

This essay argues that integration bought real macro gains without solving distribution. The defensible causal channel is narrower than the national charts: tariff-exposed local labour markets absorbed much of the cost.

1 · The Question

Goods · capital · norms

Did post-apartheid South Africa's rapid integration into world goods, capital and norms markets buy growth at the cost of distribution—and if so, through which channels (labour markets, asset prices, geography, institutions) rather than through a single headline coefficient?

In 1994 South Africa inherited an economy that had been deliberately closed off for three decades: sanctions, divestment and autarkic industrial policy had left trade at just 37% of GDP and the Johannesburg Stock Exchange largely cut off from foreign capital. The 1893 print below is not mere ornament: it marks how early South Africa was wired into imperial finance and commodity markets, so the post-1994 reopening was layered onto a long history of external price discovery and domestic concentration, not a blank slate. Two macro strategies defined the transition. The Reconstruction and Development Programme (RDP, 1994) promised redistribution, housing, electrification and a developmental state. Twenty months later it was shelved in favour of Growth, Employment and Redistribution (GEAR, 1996): a self-described orthodox macro framework with fiscal consolidation, inflation targeting, trade liberalisation, capital account opening and partial privatisation. Here, norms markets means entry into global expectations about liberalisation, credible courts, investor confidence and governance benchmarks, not merely the exchange of goods or portfolio capital.

Ordinary experience in commuter corridors, shack-side growth circles, port towns, and Gauteng influx does not show up directly in GDP per capita, yet it motivates why redistribution and geography matter politically. The Durban harbour and stack photography in §2 put physical form around the import and export ratios in §4; the Gugulethu and Cato Crest frames show that openness is lived unevenly across neighbourhoods, not as a uniform national mean. The charts and regressions test whether openness tracks output, shares and governance; causal claims cite papers in Sources alongside the correlations printed here, notably synthetic-control, local labour-market, and inequality accounting work linked below. Winning the RDP–GEAR debate was always genuinely political.

How to read this page. This is a short national time series (T ≤ 35), so the coefficients here show patterns, not proof of causation. Causal arguments in the narrative come from external studies in the sources section, especially synthetic-control work on sanctions, local labour-market evidence on tariff liberalisation, and wealth-distribution research.

2 · From the Ground

Read these photographs as a sequence, not a gallery: labour settlement, port corridor, container throughput, and the informal edge that macro averages leave behind.

01 · Labour geography

Townships before ratios

Openness does not enter a neutral labour market. Gugulethu and the hero skyline belong to the same migration economy: one registers formal opportunity, the other the queue outside it.

This is why the later macro unemployment line matters. The causal paper to keep in mind is Erten–Leight–Tregenna: tariff-exposed districts lost employment and formalisation, especially for Black workers and women.

02 · Goods corridor

The coastline becomes a denominator

Ports translate policy into movement: tariff schedules, WTO commitments, containers, rail, trucking and customs capacity all collapse into the national openness ratio.

The visual move is deliberate: before reading the line chart in §4, hold the physical corridor in view.

03 · Throughput

Boxes are not distribution

Container stacks show integration working as a flow measure. They do not tell us whether the gains became wages, jobs, profits or asset income. That distinction is the hinge between §4 and §5.

04 · Distributional edge

Return to the question

The ground story ends where the data question begins: trade can rise while employment, housing and spatial access remain uneven. The next sections test that tension with national series, then bring the causal evidence back in.

3 · Policy Timeline

The sequence matters: sanctions lift, RDP gives way to GEAR, WTO commitments bind tariff cuts, commodity capital arrives, and governance later weakens. The timeline explains when integration became policy.

    4 · Macro Evidence

    Integration, measured as (exports + imports) / GDP, more than doubled between 1990 and 2022. FDI, always modest, rode the same wave with a lag. Read the charts as a trade-off: measured openness and output rise, while labour-market absorption does not.

    From throughput to lines on a page

    Integration is visible in flows before it is visible in lives: Trade/GDP rises sharply, average income rises more modestly, and unemployment remains the stubborn counter-line.

    5 · Inequality Evidence

    Distribution is read in layers: income flows, wealth stocks, survey Gini, and spatial inequality. The order is national first, then geographic.

    Two WID ladders before the map

    The distributional story is not hidden in a small residual: by 2020 the top decile claims about 65% of pre-tax income, while the bottom half receives under 6%.

    Income The top-10% share rises from roughly 47% in 1990 to about 65% by 2020.

    Wealth Stocks are more concentrated than flows: the top decile holds roughly 85% of household wealth.

    Spatial read The map below translates the same skew into place; read it beside Gugulethu and Cato Crest.

    Source WID and Chatterjee–Czajka–Gethin.

    The panels above set the national shares; the figure below freezes one spatial slice of the same inequality geography.

    More charts: survey Gini and scatter vs trade openness

    The central puzzle shows up in the inequality data: the post-1994 openness episode did not coincide with distributional compression. From 1994 onwards, the top-decile income share rose by roughly 18 percentage points. The bottom half's share shrank by a third. Wealth is starker: the top 10% hold roughly 85% of household wealth.

    The Erten–Leight–Tregenna (2019) paper isolates the causal piece: districts more exposed to the tariff cuts of 1994–2005 saw lower employment, formalisation and wages, and the hit fell hardest on Black workers and women. That evidence, not the scatter plots in the exploratory correlation panel, is what justifies the "price of integration" framing when you move from pattern to mechanism.

    6 · Governance Evidence

    This is the norms channel: courts, accountability, rule-of-law credibility and global governance benchmarks. The series are coarse, but they test whether institutional legitimacy moved with growth.

    The WGI composite peaks around 2008 and slides during the Zuma administration (2009–2018), with a partial recovery after. This is suggestive evidence for the norms channel, not a direct causal estimate. The regression battery picks up a positive association between the WGI composite and GDP growth (β ≈ 40.5, HAC p = 0.036, n = 26), but this correlation does not identify the causal impact of state capacity on growth, especially given the short panel and the GFC shock embedded in it.

    7 · Econometric Evidence

    Summary first; full OLS tables with HAC errors and tier labels if you want every coefficient.

    Takeaways

    • Trade and top incomes move together. The relationship between trade openness and the top-1% share is the single association that survives the strictest multiple-testing correction. It is not a causal license, but a consistent pattern worth reading next to Erten–Leight–Tregenna's district evidence.
    • No clean "GEAR growth break." Chow tests around 1996 do not pick up a statistically sharp growth-regime shift at conventional levels. Alignment with the qualitative GEAR narrative is subtler.
    • Unemployment stays the brake. Macro charts and the provincial map underline that integration did not engineer a labour-market catch-up.
    • Use the tables sparingly. They are exploratory; BH / Bonf / Raw tiers flag how far each correlation survives sceptical testing.

    8 · Provincial Unemployment, 2025 Q1

    Hover a province for its narrow unemployment rate. Expanded unemployment (discouraged job-seekers included) ran at 43.1% nationally in Q1 2025. Source: Stats SA QLFS.

    National averages hide apartheid-era spatial hierarchies persisting thirty years later: former homeland regions and peri-urban belts still register the worst narrow-unemployment burdens, Gauteng notwithstanding its relative formal employment density documented above. The choropleth is a blunt instrument. Intra-provincial inequality remains large, yet it reinforces that joblessness is geographically concentrated, not uniformly distributed beneath macro openness figures. Read KwaZulu-Natal beside the Durban harbour photographs in §2, and the Western Cape beside Gugulethu—the map is the same country at administrative scale.

    9 · Conclusions

    What the 630-spec battery, the governance panel, and the cited studies show together, and what they do not.

    1 · What integration bought

    Re-entry bought macro normalisation: trade rose, sanctions faded, capital and governance signals improved, and GDP per capita ended higher than in 1994. The evidence for that gain is descriptive but visible across the macro panel.

    2 · Where distribution failed

    The gains did not become broad labour absorption or distributional compression. Top-income and wealth concentration remain extreme, and narrow unemployment is still 32.9% in Q1 2025.

    3 · The causal mechanism we can defend

    The national OLS battery shows correlations. The sharper causal claim comes from Erten–Leight–Tregenna: districts more exposed to tariff cuts saw lower employment, formalisation and wages, with the burden falling hardest on Black workers and women.

    4 · What remains uncertain

    The strongest trade/top-income association survives the correction battery, but it is still not identification. Governance also tracks growth only suggestively (n = 26), and GEAR does not produce a clean growth break in the Chow tests.

    The answer, stated cautiously, is yes: integration bought growth and macro credibility, while distributional repair failed to follow. The national series show the pattern; local labour-market evidence explains the mechanism. The next policy wave should begin there, with industrial strategy, spatial redress, and labour-market activation at the center.

    Scope of claims (short version)

    • Can claim: Post-1994 integration coincides with persistent concentration in income and wealth, plus entrenched unemployment.
    • Can claim: In this battery, the trade/top-income relationship is the most statistically durable pattern.
    • Cannot claim: Causation from this national time series alone (trade→inequality, GEAR→outcomes, governance→growth).
    • Cannot claim: Full robustness to survey-design differences and data-coverage gaps in inequality series.

    Caveats, in order of severity

    1. Small n. At most 35 annual observations. Confidence intervals are wide even when p-values look small.
    2. Autocorrelation. Many specs have Durbin-Watson below 1.5. HAC SEs partially mitigate this; the "DW < 1.5 filter" above removes those specs at the reader's request.
    3. Multiple testing. We estimate hundreds of specifications. Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-values are shown alongside raw p; a finding that survives BH is more credible.
    4. No identification. Correlations do not settle the policy debate. For causal claims the paper relies on synthetic-control (sanctions), local-labour-market designs (trade liberalisation) and distributional accounts (WIL 2021).
    5. Coverage gaps. WIID Gini drops out after 2017; WDI Gini is sparser still. Comparisons across series must account for survey design differences.
