The price of integration

When apartheid's sanctions fell away in 1994, South Africa re-entered the world economy. Three decades on, incomes are higher on average — but unemployment stays crushing across provinces, while the top 10% claim 65% of pre-tax national income. Ground-level corridors and queues sit behind those lines on a spreadsheet — charts and regressions below corroborate the story where the data allow.

1 · The question

Did post-apartheid South Africa's rapid integration into world goods, capital and norms markets buy growth at the cost of distribution?

In 1994 South Africa inherited an economy that had been deliberately closed off for three decades: sanctions, divestment and autarkic industrial policy had left trade at just 37% of GDP and the Johannesburg Stock Exchange largely cut off from foreign capital. Two macro strategies defined the transition. The Reconstruction and Development Programme (RDP, 1994) promised redistribution, housing, electrification and a developmental state. Twenty months later it was shelved in favour of Growth, Employment and Redistribution (GEAR, 1996): a self-described orthodox macro framework — fiscal consolidation, inflation targeting, trade liberalisation, capital account opening and partial privatisation.

Ordinary experience — commuter corridors, shack-side growth circles, port towns and Gauteng influx — does not show up directly in GDP per capita, yet it motivates why redistribution and geography matter politically. The charts and regressions summarise whether openness lines up with output, shares and governance; causal claims cite papers in Sources alongside the correlations printed here — not replacing them — notably synthetic-control, local labour-market, and inequality accounting work linked below. Winning the RDP–GEAR debate was always genuinely political.

How to read this page. This is a short national time series (T ≤ 35), so the coefficients here show patterns, not proof of causation. Causal arguments in the narrative come from external studies in the knowledge base — especially synthetic-control work on sanctions, local labour-market evidence on tariff liberalisation, and wealth-distribution research.

    4 · Macro evidence

    Integration, measured as (exports + imports) / GDP, more than doubled between 1990 and 2022. FDI, always modest, rode the same wave with a lag.

    Trade liberalisation was rapid: tariffs fell from an average of roughly 23% in 1993 to around 8% by 2005. In the headline OLS, a one-percentage-point increase in Trade/GDP is associated with +1.4% higher GDP per capita (HAC, p < 0.001) — but so is higher unemployment (+0.20pp, p = 0.036) and a higher top-10% income share (+0.69pp, p < 0.001). The direction of association is unambiguous; the direction of causation is not. Engle-Granger cointegration for log GDP pc and Trade/GDP fails to reject the no-cointegration null (p ≈ 0.48), so the long-run relationship should be read cautiously.

    5 · Inequality evidence

    Three complementary lenses: pre-tax income shares (WID), wealth shares (WID), and survey-based Gini (WIID / WDI).

    More charts — survey Gini and scatter vs trade openness

    The central puzzle shows up in the inequality data: opening the economy and slimming the state did not compress the distribution as classical liberal theory would predict; it widened it. From 1994 onwards, the top-decile income share rose by roughly 18 percentage points. The bottom half's share shrank by a third. Wealth is an even starker story: the top 10% hold roughly 85% of household wealth.

    The Erten–Leight–Tregenna (2019) paper isolates the causal piece: districts more exposed to the tariff cuts of 1994–2005 saw lower employment, formalisation and wages — and the hit fell hardest on Black workers and women. That evidence, not the correlations above, is what justifies the "price of integration" framing.

    6 · Governance evidence

    The six Worldwide Governance Indicators pillars, normalised to 0–1 and averaged across underlying sources.

    The WGI composite peaks around 2008 and slides during the Zuma administration (2009–2018), with a partial recovery after. The regression battery picks up a positive association between the WGI composite and GDP growth (β ≈ 40.5, HAC p = 0.036, n = 26) — but this correlation does not identify the causal impact of state capacity on growth, especially given the short panel and the GFC shock embedded in it.

    • Trade and top incomes move together. The relationship between trade openness and the top-1% share is the single association that survives the strictest multiple-testing correction — not a causal license, but a consistent pattern worth reading next to Erten–Leight–Tregenna’s district evidence.

    8 · Provincial unemployment, 2025 Q1

    Hover a province for its narrow unemployment rate. Expanded unemployment (discouraged job-seekers included) ran at 43.1% nationally in Q1 2025. Source: Stats SA QLFS.

    National averages hide apartheid-era spatial hierarchies persisting thirty years later: former homeland regions and peri-urban belts still register the worst narrow-unemployment burdens, Gauteng notwithstanding its relative formal employment density documented above. The choropleth is a blunt instrument — intra-provincial inequality remains large — yet it reinforces that joblessness is geographically concentrated, not uniformly distributed beneath macro openness figures.

    9 · Conclusions

    What the 630-spec battery, the governance panel and the knowledge base show together — and what they don't.

    1 · Integration and inequality moved together

    The single most robust relationship in the full battery is between trade openness and the top-1% income share: BH-adjusted p ≈ 10⁻⁶⁶, DW ≈ 1.65, n = 34. It is the only headline spec that survives both the DW ≥ 1.5 autocorrelation filter and Benjamini-Hochberg correction across all 630 specifications. Correlation is not causation, but this is the pattern the Erten–Leight–Tregenna (2019) local-labour-market design identifies causally at the district level.

    2 · GEAR did not produce a visible growth break

    The Chow test at the 1996 GEAR adoption year fails to reject a structural break in GDP-per-capita growth at 5% (F ≈ 1.89, p ≈ 0.168). Fiscal consolidation, partial privatisation and tariff binding delivered macro stability and a roughly one-third rise in GDP per capita — but no measurable discontinuity in the growth process itself.

    3 · Unemployment is the structural failure

    Stats SA's Q1 2025 QLFS puts narrow unemployment at 32.9% and the expanded rate at 43.1%, with youth unemployment at 46.1%. No province falls below 25%. Three decades of integration have not absorbed the labour supply that apartheid's homeland system stranded; this is the outcome on which the "price of integration" framing rests.

    4 · Governance matters, but the evidence is thin

    The WGI composite correlates positively with GDP growth (β ≈ 40.5, HAC p ≈ 0.036, n = 26), consistent with the slide in rule-of-law and control-of-corruption indicators during the Zuma era. But 26 annual observations cannot identify the causal impact of state capacity on growth, and the global financial crisis sits inside the sample. Treat this as suggestive, not conclusive.

    The honest reading is that South Africa bought macro stability and a modest rise in average incomes at the price of a more extreme distribution of those incomes, and without solving the employment problem the transition inherited. That trade-off is what the next wave of policy — industrial strategy, spatial redress, labour-market activation — has to take as its starting point.

    Scope of claims (short version)

    • Can claim: Post-1994 integration coincides with persistent concentration in income and wealth, plus entrenched unemployment.
    • Can claim: In this battery, the trade/top-income relationship is the most statistically durable pattern.
    • Cannot claim: Causation from this national time series alone (trade→inequality, GEAR→outcomes, governance→growth).
    • Cannot claim: Full robustness to survey-design differences and data-coverage gaps in inequality series.

    Caveats, in order of severity

    1. Small n. At most 35 annual observations. Confidence intervals are wide even when p-values look small.
    2. Autocorrelation. Many specs have Durbin-Watson below 1.5. HAC SEs partially mitigate this; the "DW < 1.5 filter" above removes those specs at the reader's request.
    3. Multiple testing. We estimate hundreds of specifications. Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-values are shown alongside raw p; a finding that survives BH is more credible.
    4. No identification. Correlations do not settle the policy debate. For causal claims the paper relies on synthetic-control (sanctions), local-labour-market designs (trade liberalisation) and distributional accounts (WIL 2021).
    5. Coverage gaps. WIID Gini drops out after 2017; WDI Gini is sparser still. Comparisons across series must account for survey design differences.
