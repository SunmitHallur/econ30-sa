The Price of Integration

When apartheid's sanctions fell away in 1994, South Africa re-entered the world economy. Three decades on, incomes are higher on average, but unemployment stays crushing across provinces, while the top 10% claim 65% of pre-tax national income. The Braamfontein skyline beside this page is the same Gauteng magnet that pulls migrants while national joblessness stays structurally high; the small chart pairs openness with concentration so you can hold the distributional question in view before the long evidence chain. Ground-level corridors, port cranes, and settlement edges sit behind those lines on a spreadsheet—photographs and series below are meant to answer the same question from different distances.

Goods · capital · norms

1 · The Question

Did post-apartheid South Africa's rapid integration into world goods, capital and norms markets buy growth at the cost of distribution—and if so, through which channels (labour markets, asset prices, geography, institutions) rather than through a single headline coefficient?

In 1994 South Africa inherited an economy that had been deliberately closed off for three decades: sanctions, divestment and autarkic industrial policy had left trade at just 37% of GDP and the Johannesburg Stock Exchange largely cut off from foreign capital. The 1893 print below is not mere ornament: it marks how early South Africa was wired into imperial finance and commodity markets, so the post-1994 reopening was layered onto a long history of external price discovery and domestic concentration—not a blank slate. Two macro strategies defined the transition. The Reconstruction and Development Programme (RDP, 1994) promised redistribution, housing, electrification and a developmental state. Twenty months later it was shelved in favour of Growth, Employment and Redistribution (GEAR, 1996): a self-described orthodox macro framework with fiscal consolidation, inflation targeting, trade liberalisation, capital account opening and partial privatisation.

Ordinary experience in commuter corridors, shack-side growth circles, port towns, and Gauteng influx does not show up directly in GDP per capita, yet it motivates why redistribution and geography matter politically. The Durban harbour and stack photography in §2 is the physical face of the import and export ratios you will see in §4; the Gugulethu and Cato Crest frames remind you that openness is felt unevenly across neighbourhoods, not as a uniform national mean. The charts and regressions summarise whether openness lines up with output, shares and governance; causal claims cite papers in Sources alongside the correlations printed here, not replacing them, notably synthetic-control, local labour-market, and inequality accounting work linked below. Winning the RDP–GEAR debate was always genuinely political.

How to read this page. This is a short national time series (T ≤ 35), so the coefficients here show patterns, not proof of causation. Causal arguments in the narrative come from external studies in the sources section, especially synthetic-control work on sanctions, local labour-market evidence on tariff liberalisation, and wealth-distribution research.

    4 · Macro Evidence

    Integration, measured as (exports + imports) / GDP, more than doubled between 1990 and 2022. FDI, always modest, rode the same wave with a lag. The charts are spaced here so you can pause on each line before reading the regression paragraph.

    From throughput to lines on a page

    National openness ratios stay abstract until you pair them with a berth full of boxes. When Trade/GDP climbs in the indexed panel, picture the gantry cranes and yard reach stackers from §2: the same integration episode lifts measured flows while labour-market and distributional outcomes can diverge sharply by district and skill group.

    Indexed macro (log scale). GDP per capita, Trade/GDP and FDI/GDP are rebased to 1990 = 100. The log y-axis keeps GDP’s roughly one-third rise legible next to the much larger swings in openness and FDI. Source: World Development Indicators.

    Unemployment (ILO-modelled). The plateau is the defining macro fact beneath the openness story. Cross-check with QLFS and the labour-market notes in Sources.

    Trade liberalisation was rapid: tariffs fell from an average of roughly 23% in 1993 to around 8% by 2005. In the headline OLS, a one-percentage-point increase in Trade/GDP is associated with +1.4% higher GDP per capita (HAC, p < 0.001), but so is higher unemployment (+0.20pp, p = 0.036) and a higher top-10% income share (+0.69pp, p < 0.001). The direction of association is unambiguous; the direction of causation is not. Engle-Granger cointegration for log GDP pc and Trade/GDP fails to reject the no-cointegration null (p ≈ 0.48), so the long-run relationship should be read cautiously.

    5 · Inequality Evidence

    Three complementary lenses: pre-tax income shares (WID), wealth shares (WID), and survey-based Gini (WIID / WDI)—with geography brought in after the aggregate curves so you can read the map as a spatial translation of the same skew.

    Two WID ladders before the map

    Pre-tax income shares. The top 10% of adults claim about 47% of pre-tax national income in 1990 and about 65% by 2020, while the bottom half receives under 6%. The curve is a national compression of every pay negotiation and dividend flow in the sample.

    Wealth shares. The stock view is starker than the flow view: concentration in asset markets and housing stock matters for persistence. See Chatterjee-Czajka-Gethin (WIL, 2021) for how the top decile’s wealth share sits versus the bottom half.

    The decile lines on the right compress the country into a single ladder; the choropleth further down makes the same skew legible in space (Cape Town inset, generalized entropy layer)—coastal metros, former homeland boundaries, and peri-urban rings colour differently even when survey Gini barely moves year to year. Treat that map as a companion to the Gugulethu and Cato Crest photographs: satellite distance versus street distance.

    The panels above set the national shares; the figure below freezes one spatial slice of the same inequality geography.

    More charts: survey Gini and scatter vs trade openness

    The central puzzle shows up in the inequality data: opening the economy and slimming the state did not compress the distribution as classical liberal theory would predict; it widened it. From 1994 onwards, the top-decile income share rose by roughly 18 percentage points. The bottom half's share shrank by a third. Wealth is an even starker story: the top 10% hold roughly 85% of household wealth.

    The Erten–Leight–Tregenna (2019) paper isolates the causal piece: districts more exposed to the tariff cuts of 1994–2005 saw lower employment, formalisation and wages, and the hit fell hardest on Black workers and women. That evidence, not the scatter plots in the exploratory correlation panel, is what justifies the "price of integration" framing when you move from pattern to mechanism.

    6 · Governance Evidence

    The six Worldwide Governance Indicators pillars, normalised to 0–1 and averaged across underlying sources.

    The WGI composite peaks around 2008 and slides during the Zuma administration (2009–2018), with a partial recovery after. The regression battery picks up a positive association between the WGI composite and GDP growth (β ≈ 40.5, HAC p = 0.036, n = 26), but this correlation does not identify the causal impact of state capacity on growth, especially given the short panel and the GFC shock embedded in it.

    7 · Econometric Evidence

    Summary first; full OLS tables with HAC errors and tier labels if you want every coefficient.

    Takeaways

    • Trade and top incomes move together. The relationship between trade openness and the top-1% share is the single association that survives the strictest multiple-testing correction. It is not a causal license, but a consistent pattern worth reading next to Erten-Leight-Tregenna's district evidence.
    • No clean "GEAR growth break." Chow tests around 1996 do not pick up a statistically sharp growth-regime shift at conventional levels. Alignment with the qualitative GEAR narrative is subtler.
    • Unemployment stays the brake. Macro charts and the provincial map underline that integration did not engineer a labour-market catch-up.
    • Use the tables sparingly. They are exploratory; BH / Bonf / Raw tiers flag how far each correlation survives sceptical testing.

    8 · Provincial Unemployment, 2025 Q1

    Hover a province for its narrow unemployment rate. Expanded unemployment (discouraged job-seekers included) ran at 43.1% nationally in Q1 2025. Source: Stats SA QLFS.

    National averages hide apartheid-era spatial hierarchies persisting thirty years later: former homeland regions and peri-urban belts still register the worst narrow-unemployment burdens, Gauteng notwithstanding its relative formal employment density documented above. The choropleth is a blunt instrument. Intra-provincial inequality remains large, yet it reinforces that joblessness is geographically concentrated, not uniformly distributed beneath macro openness figures. Read KwaZulu-Natal beside the Durban harbour photographs in §2, and the Western Cape beside Gugulethu—the map is the same country at administrative scale.

    9 · Conclusions

    What the 630-spec battery, the governance panel, and the cited studies show together, and what they do not.

    1 · Integration and inequality moved together

    The single most robust relationship in the full battery is between trade openness and the top-1% income share: BH-adjusted p ≈ 10⁻⁶⁶, DW ≈ 1.65, n = 34. It is the only headline spec that survives both the DW ≥ 1.5 autocorrelation filter and Benjamini-Hochberg correction across all 630 specifications. Correlation is not causation, but this is the pattern the Erten–Leight–Tregenna (2019) local-labour-market design identifies causally at the district level.

    2 · GEAR did not produce a visible growth break

    The Chow test at the 1996 GEAR adoption year fails to reject a structural break in GDP-per-capita growth at 5% (F ≈ 1.89, p ≈ 0.168). Fiscal consolidation, partial privatisation and tariff binding delivered macro stability and a roughly one-third rise in GDP per capita, but no measurable discontinuity in the growth process itself.

    3 · Unemployment is the structural failure

    Stats SA's Q1 2025 QLFS puts narrow unemployment at 32.9% and the expanded rate at 43.1%, with youth unemployment at 46.1%. No province falls below 25%. Three decades of integration have not absorbed the labour supply that apartheid's homeland system stranded; this is the outcome on which the "price of integration" framing rests.

    4 · Governance matters, but the evidence is thin

    The WGI composite correlates positively with GDP growth (β ≈ 40.5, HAC p ≈ 0.036, n = 26), consistent with the slide in rule-of-law and control-of-corruption indicators during the Zuma era. But 26 annual observations cannot identify the causal impact of state capacity on growth, and the global financial crisis sits inside the sample. Treat this as suggestive, not conclusive.

    Opening to trade brought South Africa increased macroeconomic stability and a modest rise in average incomes. However, this came at the price of higher income inequality and severe domestic employment pressures. The next wave of policy should start from that reality, with industrial strategy, spatial redress, and labour market activation at the center.

    Scope of claims (short version)

    • Can claim: Post-1994 integration coincides with persistent concentration in income and wealth, plus entrenched unemployment.
    • Can claim: In this battery, the trade/top-income relationship is the most statistically durable pattern.
    • Cannot claim: Causation from this national time series alone (trade→inequality, GEAR→outcomes, governance→growth).
    • Cannot claim: Full robustness to survey-design differences and data-coverage gaps in inequality series.

    Caveats, in order of severity

    1. Small n. At most 35 annual observations. Confidence intervals are wide even when p-values look small.
    2. Autocorrelation. Many specs have Durbin-Watson below 1.5. HAC SEs partially mitigate this; the "DW < 1.5 filter" above removes those specs at the reader's request.
    3. Multiple testing. We estimate hundreds of specifications. Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-values are shown alongside raw p; a finding that survives BH is more credible.
    4. No identification. Correlations do not settle the policy debate. For causal claims the paper relies on synthetic-control (sanctions), local-labour-market designs (trade liberalisation) and distributional accounts (WIL 2021).
    5. Coverage gaps. WIID Gini drops out after 2017; WDI Gini is sparser still. Comparisons across series must account for survey design differences.
